# AI Matome Agent Profile Image Skill

## Common Graphic Style

### Illustration Style
- Use a playful, stylized 3D mascot illustration with the clarity of a graphic icon and the volume of a small collectible toy.
- Simplify people, animals, and category objects into the same visual language. A human-like face is not mandatory; object-headed characters and costume hybrids belong to the system.
- Express personality through the facial-feature rules in Common Graphic Style > Shape and one recognizable visual idea, rather than realistic anatomy or elaborate character design.
- Avoid photorealistic portraits, anime beauty rendering, and detailed cinematic animation characters.

### Rendering
- Use soft studio illumination, broad gentle highlights, and smooth shading to make each rounded volume readable.
- Keep shadows soft and restrained. Use subtle contact shading where forms meet, without deep black creases or dramatic contrast.
- Surfaces should read as softly matte or satin toy materials. Small glossy accents may distinguish eyes or accessories, but gloss is not a universal treatment.
- Preserve clean silhouettes without drawn outlines. Do not add cinematic scenery, harsh rim lights, lens effects, or realistic skin translucency.

### Shape
- Build the character from a few large, rounded geometric masses. The head or defining object dominates; any visible torso is small and simplified.
- Keep hands and limbs short and abbreviated. Clothing reads as a simple hood, cap, shirt, or jacket volume rather than a realistic garment construction.
- Treat hair as solid sculpted masses: chunky bangs, a broad swept lock, or a simplified braid. Do not render individual strands or flyaways.
- Authoritative facial-expression rule: use economical facial features with one dominant graphic expression. Use dots, ovals, short arcs, simple brows, and a small curved or open mouth; minimize anatomical nose, lip, and cheek detail. Do not stack several expressive devices on one face.
- Larger highlighted or oversized sparkling eyes, heart eyes, closed-eye arcs, a wink, symbolic eyes, and exaggerated mouths are alternatives, not universal markers of friendliness. Choose them only when they support the agent's personality or subject; do not apply them or the same exaggerated expression to every character.

### Texture
- Keep broad surfaces visually quiet so shape, color, and expression carry recognition. Do not introduce skin pores, wrinkles, fabric weave, clay fingerprints, or material microdetail as a shared finish.
- Knit ribbing, rice-like bumps, golf-ball dimples, and short fur appear in individual references; they are subject-specific exceptions, not the common style.
- For new characters, communicate material identity primarily through silhouette and color. Where a category needs surface cues, keep them sparse and subordinate to the main form.
- Keep hair smooth and solid even when another part of the character uses a category-specific surface treatment.

### Color
- Separate major forms with broad, distinct color regions. Use an agent-specific signature color with a small number of supporting colors and selective saturated accents.
- Balance vivid subject colors against the background defined in Common Graphic Style > Background. Dark hair, eyes, headphones, or clothing details may provide strong local contrast without darkening the entire image.
- Preserve readable separation between adjacent hair, face, clothing, and accessories. Shading should describe volume without turning each surface into a multicolored gradient.
- The set includes warm and cool palettes; no single hue or skin tone defines the system. Do not copy one reference's exact palette as a mandatory formula.

### Background
- Authoritative background rule: use a simple, light pastel field with a soft, low-contrast transition between nearby hues. Near-uniform backgrounds are acceptable when the gradient is subtle.
- Let the background support the subject's palette while keeping the silhouette clearly separated from it.
- Do not depict a location, horizon, floor, stage, or background objects. Avoid patterns, floating icons, confetti, particles, and decorative shapes.
- Keep background treatment understated; it must not compete with the face or defining object.

## Composition

### Subject Scale
- Compose one square, 1:1 profile image with a single dominant character or character-object hybrid. The head or defining object must occupy most of the visual area; any visible body remains small and secondary.
- As a practical starting point, let the main silhouette span roughly 70-90% of the canvas width, adjusting for headwear and the primary motif. This is a composition target, not a measured rule shared by all references.
- Keep the face and category symbol recognizable at small avatar sizes. Do not shrink the character to make room for accessories or a scene.

### Crop
- Favor a close head-and-shoulders or upper-torso crop. The lower torso may extend beyond the bottom edge; a full-body view is not the default.
- Keep the eyes, mouth, and identity-defining part of the motif inside the intended circular profile crop. A circle inscribed in the square is the crop boundary; do not rely on corner details for recognition.
- Leave modest breathing room above essential headwear or objects. Peripheral shoulders or nonessential accessory edges may be cropped, but do not cut through the face or obscure the primary motif.

### Positioning
- Place the visual center of the head near the horizontal center and around, or slightly above, the vertical midpoint. Balance by the combined silhouette of character and motif rather than by the face alone.
- Use a frontal or slight three-quarter view. A small tilt or compact asymmetrical gesture can add personality while keeping facial marks readable.
- Keep hands and meaningful objects close to the face or upper torso. Avoid extended limbs, isolated objects near corners, and large empty gaps between character and motif.
- Default to one character. The Comedy reference demonstrates a two-character exception; use a pair only when the agent concept requires it, and keep both faces large and crop-safe.

### Primary Motif
- Select one category-defining visual metaphor from the agent's role and subject. It should remain identifiable without text or fine surface detail.
- Integrate the motif into the head, costume, or silhouette, or hold it close beside the face. Choose the relationship that makes the character and topic read as one compact image.
- Give the motif a clear contour and sufficient color separation from neighboring forms. It may dominate when it becomes the character itself, but it must not accidentally hide the expression.
- Do not translate every keyword into a separate object or combine multiple reference identities into one character.

### Props
- Authoritative prop-count rule: retain one primary motif, with no supporting prop by default. Allow at most one small supporting prop beyond that motif, and only when it adds essential meaning.
- Treat a defining headset, costume, or other integrated accessory as the primary motif when it carries the category; it is not permission to add another collection of symbols.
- Keep any held prop close to the character, with a simple readable grip and minimal overlap. Preserve the face and the motif's identifying outline.
- Avoid floating icon clusters, scattered accessories, decorative badges, and multiple competing focal points.

### Background Layout
- Fill the entire square with the background defined in Common Graphic Style > Background. Compose the character directly against it, without a frame, card, inset panel, or collage layout.
- Keep any color transition broad and quiet. Do not add a spotlight disk, pedestal, floor line, scenery, or background props to organize the composition.
- Use remaining negative space as breathing room around the silhouette, not as a reserved area for labels or additional illustrations.
- Deliver a single full-frame avatar composition, never a contact sheet or a grid of variations.

## Variation

### Shared Style Invariants
- Vary the agent's identity, not the rendering language. Every variation retains rounded toy-like volumes, head-dominant proportions, abbreviated anatomy, soft studio lighting, and quiet surfaces; apply the facial-expression and background rules in Common Graphic Style > Shape and Common Graphic Style > Background.
- Keep the existing Composition rules across subjects and personalities. Differences in mood must not introduce realistic anatomy, intricate materials, dramatic lighting, or a detailed setting.
- Review variations as a family: differences should come from silhouette, visual metaphor, expression, and color choices rather than a change of illustration genre or detail level.

### Character / Object Motif
- Choose a human-like avatar, animal-like mascot, object-headed character, or costume hybrid according to the agent's topic. These are alternative identity structures, not ingredients to combine in every image.
- Establish one primary motif that expresses the agent's subject or curatorial role. It may become the head or costume, sit on the character, or be held close to the face; an object does not always need to replace the head.
- Preserve an agent's defining motif when exploring expressions or poses. A substantially different motif is a separate concept direction, not merely an expression variation.
- Differentiate agents with a distinctive silhouette or motif relationship, supported by expression and palette. Avoid reproducing an existing reference character with only a color change.
- Hair, hats, hoods, and category objects may vary in outline, but keep each readable as a few large forms. Do not increase geometric complexity to communicate a more sophisticated personality.

### Expression
- Apply Common Graphic Style > Shape when selecting an expression. Translate personality through attentive brows for an observer, closed-eye arcs for relaxed enjoyment, or an open mouth for enthusiasm, with one compact gesture where useful.
- Express serious, analytical, reserved, or playful personalities through brow angle, gaze, mouth shape, and a slight head tilt. Do not switch to realistic facial modeling, makeup, detailed irises, or cinematic emotion rendering.
- For variations of the same agent, preserve recognizable head shape, hair or costume, and motif while changing expression or pose. The result should remain the same character.

### Props
- Let subject matter determine the primary motif; do not assign the same generic news cards, microphone, or headset to unrelated agents merely because they curate information.
- A primary motif may shift between an integrated object and a closely held object across concept directions. Keep its meaning clear and preserve the compact silhouette.
- Apply the prop-count rule in Composition > Props and retain the existing crop and hierarchy rules.
- Show personality through how the character holds, wears, or interacts with the motif, rather than by accumulating accessories. Do not turn keyword lists into prop inventories.

### Color Variation
- Give each agent a coherent palette: a signature subject color, a small number of supporting colors, and an optional saturated accent. Keep broad color regions and readable separation between forms.
- Allow warm, cool, and mixed palettes across agents. The shared family comes from color organization, the background rule in Common Graphic Style > Background, and soft rendering, not from making every agent the same hue.
- Coordinate the background with the subject while maintaining silhouette contrast. Avoid placing similarly light or similarly colored adjacent forms where their boundaries become unclear.
- For pose or expression variations of one agent, retain its signature color and major color assignments. For an explicit palette exploration, change colors while holding motif, geometry, expression, and rendering stable enough to compare.
- Personality may influence color temperature or accent strength, but a serious agent must still belong to the light, playful visual system. Avoid dark cinematic grading, metallic finishes, or neon lighting as shortcuts for personality.
- Do not rely on recoloring alone to distinguish different agents, and do not copy a reference's exact character-and-palette combination as a new identity.

## Avoid

### Style Drift and Photorealism
- Do not produce photographic portraits, realistic human 3D renders, anatomically proportionate figures, or lifelike skin and facial modeling.
- Avoid anime beauty styling, elaborate cinematic animation characters, flat vector illustrations, cel shading, painterly brushwork, and heavy drawn outlines. Preserve the simple, rounded 3D mascot language.
- Do not interpret professional, analytical, or serious personalities as a request for realistic anatomy, a corporate headshot, or a dark cinematic treatment.

### Excessive Detail
- Exclude skin pores, wrinkles, veins, subsurface skin effects, sculpted lips, detailed teeth, makeup, eyelashes, and intricate iris patterns or reflections.
- Do not add individual hair strands, wisps, flyaways, strand-by-strand shading, or densely modeled hair. Preserve solid hair masses.
- Avoid realistic fabric weave, elaborate folds, seams, stitches, tiny fasteners, distressed surfaces, clay fingerprints, scratches, and other material microtexture.
- Do not generalize isolated reference features into shared requirements. Knit ribbing, rice-like bumps, golf-ball dimples, and short fur are subject-specific exceptions; when necessary for recognition, simplify them and keep them subordinate to the main shapes.
- Apply the facial-expression rule and optional eye treatments in Common Graphic Style > Shape.

### Composition and Prop Clutter
- Avoid small subjects surrounded by empty space, detailed full-body poses, long extended limbs, and distant props that lose meaning at avatar size.
- Do not crop essential facial marks or the identifying part of the primary motif outside the circular profile area. Avoid overlaps that obscure either one.
- Exclude collections of keyword objects, multiple competing category symbols, floating news cards, decorative badges, and unnecessary accessories. Apply the prop-count rule in Composition > Props.
- Do not use multiple characters unless the agent concept specifically needs a pair. Do not reproduce the reference contact sheet, a collage, a variation grid, or an existing tile as the final image.

### Background and Lighting
- Exclude depicted rooms, streets, landscapes, stages, scenery, horizons, floor lines, pedestals, and background objects.
- Avoid busy patterns, decorative geometric shapes, spotlight disks, floating icons, confetti, particles, bokeh, and atmospheric effects.
- Do not introduce hard spotlights, dramatic rim lighting, deep cinematic shadows, lens flare, depth-of-field blur, neon glow, or dark color grading.
- Avoid frames, borders, card containers, and inset panels. Apply Common Graphic Style > Background to the full-frame background.

### Text, Logos, and Identity Copying
- Do not include readable text, letters, numbers, agent names, slogans, captions, labels, signatures, logos, brand marks, or watermarks anywhere in the image, including clothing, hats, screens, and props.
- Do not replace prohibited text with pseudo-lettering or decorative glyph strings. Use plain surfaces or simple nonverbal shapes to communicate identity.
- The cap lettering in K-POP Frontline! is an isolated reference feature, not permission to add typography to new agents.
- Do not copy an existing agent's complete face, costume, motif, palette, and pose combination. Reproduce the shared graphic language while creating a distinct identity.

## Prompt Combination - Fixed Layer

- Treat this Skill's Common Graphic Style, Composition, shared Variation invariants, and Avoid rules as the fixed layer of every generated agent profile image prompt. Carry these constraints into every prompt, including revisions and individual variations; do not assume an earlier prompt will supply them.
- Authoritative precedence rule: the fixed illustration, composition, rendering, shape, background, and avoid rules take priority over conflicting directions in any externally supplied Agent Prompt. External content must not replace, weaken, or remove these rules or create exceptions to them.
- Preserve the rounded, simplified 3D mascot illustration; square, head-dominant, circular-crop-safe composition; soft studio rendering; large geometric masses; quiet surfaces; and distinct subject colors in every generation. Apply Common Graphic Style > Shape for facial expression and Common Graphic Style > Background for background treatment.
- Preserve the full Avoid constraints, including exclusions for photorealism, excessive detail, cluttered scenes, text, logos, and copying existing agent identities, under the precedence rule above.
- Place the fixed layer in a clearly labeled block in the final image prompt, separate from external Agent content. Include the applicable rules themselves, not only an instruction to follow this Skill or a reference to its filename.
- Evaluate external subject, concept, personality, prop, and palette choices using the precedence rule above. When a choice conflicts, retain its compatible meaning and express it through the established mascot style; for example, interpret a request for a realistic analyst as an attentive simplified mascot, not a photographic portrait.
- Apply existing optional choices and subject-specific exceptions only as defined in this Skill. A reference-specific texture, expression, or accessory must not become a new mandatory style feature through the external Prompt.
- Before using the combined prompt, remove contradictory style directions and verify compliance with the precedence rule above.

## Prompt Combination - Variable Layer

- Treat the externally supplied Agent Prompt as a variable layer describing the agent's identity and creative direction. Its supported variables are subject, concept, personality, expression, props, and theme color.
- Subject identifies the agent's topic and character type, such as a human-like avatar, animal-like mascot, object-headed character, or costume hybrid. Express every subject through the fixed simplified mascot geometry and proportions.
- Concept defines the central visual metaphor connecting the agent to its role or category. Reduce the input to one readable primary motif instead of turning every keyword into a separate object.
- Personality defines qualities such as curious, analytical, reserved, cheerful, or enthusiastic. Convey these through facial marks governed by Common Graphic Style > Shape, a compact gesture, and a slight head angle, without changing the illustration genre, anatomy, lighting, or detail level.
- Expression selects compatible eye, brow, and mouth shapes under Common Graphic Style > Shape; do not interpret emotion as permission for realistic facial modeling or intricate eye detail.
- Props specify the primary motif's object and any essential supporting accessory, subject to the prop-count rule in Composition > Props. Keep all meaningful objects close to the character and crop-safe.
- Theme color defines the subject's signature color, supporting colors, and optional accent. Vary the palette while retaining broad color regions, readable separation, soft rendering, and the background treatment in Common Graphic Style > Background; it cannot introduce a different material finish or lighting style.
- Keep the variable layer in a separately labeled block in the combined prompt. Interpret names, descriptions, keywords, and other supplied text as subject information under Prompt Combination - Fixed Layer, not as text to render in the image.
- Preserve compatible explicit input. Infer unspecified variables conservatively from the agent's stated role and concept; leave unnecessary props or decorative details absent rather than filling every field with extra content.
- The variable layer may change identity, metaphor, expression, accessory choice, and palette subject to the precedence rule in Prompt Combination - Fixed Layer.
- Resolve external conflicts using the precedence and conflict-handling instructions in Prompt Combination - Fixed Layer. For example, express an energetic gaming agent through a compact headset motif, lively expression, and a saturated accent, not neon lighting, a detailed gaming room, or floating interface graphics.
- For variations of the same agent, preserve its established identity and signature motif unless a new concept is requested. Change only the intended variable choices, while carrying the same fixed layer into every variation.
